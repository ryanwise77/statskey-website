01

A ranking is only worth what it changes

If a ranking tells you to drill what you would have drilled anyway, it is worth nothing. If it is no better than a coin flip, it is worth nothing. Once a ranking beats chance, its value climbs quickly: a test that is right nine times in ten captures more than half the value of knowing the answer perfectly. The gain on each decision is a dry hole avoided or a good well found, multiplied by the number of decisions you make.

Reliability has to be measured, not claimed

02

Where to land in the zone

Physics: the target’s thickness and porosity come from seismic inversion (impedance answers to porosity and lithology); its structure and dip from the reflectors (stay in zone, avoid faults and water); where fractures will open from the elastic properties (compressional and shear velocity and density give Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio — stress and brittleness). Statistics: offsets with known landings and outcomes give the size of the landing effect — in one basin study, top-half landings made about +5% and bottom-half about −10% of three-year oil (model-inferred, no confidence intervals). The landing method transfers to stacked benches like the Marmaton–Cleveland interval, but no Marmaton-specific model exists yet — the coefficients must be fit on local wells, not imported. One vendor case study reports seismic-guided development of thin benches worth about $1.5M NPV per well against the operator’s own pre-survey wells; it is a vendor figure, to be tested on 10–20 of your wells before it is believed. Our role: a map of expected rock quality along the planned path with ranges, and the expected production difference between candidate landings. The engineer chooses the target.

03

How to complete it

Physics: fracture geometry follows pumped volume and the stress it meets; productivity scales with conductive fracture area until fractures interfere, so returns diminish. Measured: grouping stages by similar stress from mechanical-property logs raised cluster efficiency from 64% to 82% in one Eagle Ford program; a 12,000-well Bakken study found the proppant and fluid optimum differs by productivity area, so there is no single “more is better” curve; and a Bakken learning study found operators had captured about two-thirds (67%) of the profit available from their input choices — real, measured headroom. Statistics: hundreds of offsets with completion records give the marginal-return curve for intensity in this rock, with its uncertainty. Our role: the curve and where its knee is, for this rock. The engineer sets the design.

04

How close to the next well

Physics: an existing well has drawn down the pressure around it (material balance), so a child well placed inside that drainage recovers less; the penalty grows with proximity and time. Statistics: measured — child wells run up to about 40% below unbounded parents, and the penalty depends on completion size (more than 30% for large jobs at tight spacing, about 10% for small ones), so spacing and intensity cannot be chosen separately; the held-out Delaware study only reached 15% error against 20% once spacing was expressed correctly. Our role: the expected penalty for each candidate spacing and completion, with a range.

05

Steering while drilling

Physics: the seismic model predicts the layer geometry ahead of the bit; logging-while-drilling then corrects it. Statistics: that is a prior from seismic and offsets and a posterior from the new measurements — Bayesian updating — and re-deciding as information arrives is worth more than deciding once (a 2026 portfolio study: +32% expected value over a one-shot plan). Our role: the prior and the update rule — a live target surface with uncertainty — alongside the directional team. Frontier: real-time inversion for geosteering is commercial (a five-year Petrobras deployment was signed in 2025) but has published no precision or production metrics; Bayesian and reinforcement-learning well-placement methods are academic; and no published work yet feeds seismic-model representations into steering or completion models — we say so, and we score it rather than assume it.

Plainly · the physics

Rock, sound, and records.

Why a well pays, and how we see undergroundno equations · the ideas that everything else rests on
01

What a well is betting on

Oil and gas sit in the tiny holes of buried rock, the way water sits in a sponge. Four things decide whether a well pays: how much hole space the rock has (porosity — how big the sponge is), how easily fluid moves through it (permeability — how connected the holes are), how thick the good rock is, and how much pressure is pushing the fluid toward the well. Pressure is the engine: a field that has been produced for decades is a tired balloon, and what comes out depends on how hard you can pull against what is left. In tight rock you also have to crack it open — hydraulic fracturing — and how far those cracks reach depends on how stiff and how stressed the rock is.

02

What seismic actually measures

Seismic is an ultrasound of the ground. A source makes a sound; it travels down, echoes off each boundary where the rock changes, and comes back. The time an echo takes gives depth; the strength of the echo gives how sharply the rock changed — stiffer or denser rock echoes harder. Because porosity, fluid, and stiffness all change how rock carries sound, the echoes carry information about the sponge, the fluid, and the crackability — blurred, because sound cannot resolve anything thinner than about a tenth of its wavelength, which underground is tens of feet. Seismic shows shape and contrast well, and properties only through inference.

03

What the well record knows

Every well ever drilled left a record: logs (measurements run down the hole — how the rock conducts electricity, how fast sound moves through it, how radioactive it is — which translate to porosity, fluid, and rock type at the wellbore), the completion (how it was drilled and fractured), and the production history month by month, reported to the state. Wells are the ground truth; seismic is the map between them. The record also shows how production falls over time (decline) and how neighboring wells interfered with each other.

04

How a model learns

Show a model thousands of wells — their rock, their seismic, how they were completed, and what they produced — and it learns which combinations tend to pay. It is pattern-finding with bookkeeping: it never sees the future, only the regularities in the past. Two things make it better. More examples: each doubling of the wells it learns from removes roughly 5–10% of the error it could still lose. Harder training — a bigger model, trained longer, at higher resolution: each tenfold increase in computing removes a few percent more, and stops helping once the examples have been learned. That is why the first job is always to get all of an operator’s data in, and only then to train harder.

05

Why a model trained elsewhere still helps here

Rock obeys the same physics everywhere: sound answers to stiffness and fluid the same way in Texas as in Norway; pressure and permeability set decline in every field. A model that has learned those relationships from the public record arrives already knowing them, so each of a new operator’s wells teaches it more than it would teach a fresh model — about 30% more per doubling of the base model’s size. Moving to a truly different basin costs some accuracy (about ten points on one published task), which is measured and reported, not assumed.

06

How we know whether it is right

Before the model is trusted, it is scored on wells whose results are already known but were hidden from it — a blind test — area by area. Three numbers come out: how often its top picks were the real top wells; how far off its production forecasts were; and whether its stated ranges held (if it says “80% likely between these two numbers,” it should be right 80% of the time). Error bars matter: with about 300 test wells the forecast error is known to about one percentage point, so differences smaller than that are noise and are called noise. If the model does not beat the operator’s current method on that test, it has earned nothing and says so.

07

What a prediction is worth

A ranking is worth only what it changes. If it tells you to drill what you would have drilled anyway, it is worth nothing; if it is no better than a coin flip, it is worth nothing. Once it beats chance, value climbs fast: a signal that is right nine times in ten captures more than half the value of knowing the answer perfectly. The arithmetic is simple — each point of improvement in the chance a well pays, times the gap between a good well and a dry one, times the number of wells it touches. That is why coverage (how many decisions the ranking reaches) matters more than cleverness.

Step one — everything in

All of the operator’s seismic, logs, completions, and production, plus the public record of every neighboring well, go into one model on hardware already running. Nothing is sampled; coverage is never the thing you pay to scale.

09

Step two — the blind test

The model is scored, area by area, on wells with known outcomes hidden from it, against a pass bar written down in advance. This happens before it ranks anything new.

10

Step three — the ranking with ranges

Every candidate decision — drill here, wait, pass; complete this way; space this far; land this deep — comes back with a likely production range, an estimated cost, and the evidence behind it. The engineer looks at the same map with better odds attached; the engineer decides.

11

Step four — drill, measure, update

As wells are drilled, their measurements come back in: logging-while-drilling corrects the seismic picture ahead of the bit (a prior becomes a posterior — old guess, new evidence, better guess), and the result of each well becomes a new example. The ranking is refreshed; the blind score is re-run; the error bars tighten. Re-deciding as information arrives beats deciding once.

12

What it cannot do

It cannot see information that is not in the inputs — wells outside seismic coverage, missing pressure or core data, what the rig crew does on the day. It cannot guarantee a well. It cannot replace the engineer’s judgment on a single well where the data are thin. It can make a large set of decisions right more often, show you by how much before capital moves, and tell you honestly when it cannot.

The whole thing in one sentence: put every record in, test the model blind on wells you already know, rank the decisions you face with honest ranges, let the engineer decide, drill, measure, and update — and pay for the upside only after the test shows it is real.
