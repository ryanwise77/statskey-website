A useful signal for production screening

The model predicts a 90-day spell with no reported oil or gas production. On the future-time test, it outperformed the event rate observed in the training data.

The paired loss difference is −0.3204; its nominal 95% clustered interval is −0.3352 to −0.3064, excluding zero. This is an unadjusted result against a simple baseline, not independent commercial validation.

This percentage measures prediction loss—not accuracy, avoided shutdowns, or return on investment. A well can restart after the recorded event; this label does not establish abandonment or depleted reserves.

How to read the statistics

Production evidence. The zero-production result uses 8,027 entity clusters and 298,162 repeated entity/date/horizon rows. The interval uses 500 whole-entity bootstrap resamples. Those rows are not independent wells.

The intervals are nominal: they do not adjust for multiple comparisons, geographic dependence between entities, or changes in future operating conditions. The comparator is a simple event-rate prior, not a stronger model of each well’s production history.

Coverage and independence. Training-eligible operating areas include Raton, San Juan, and Uinta. Texas Panhandle, Eastern Shelf, and Green River remain missing from that training scope.

Area and basin tests share the same test identities. The operator test overlaps training entities. Four reported protocols therefore do not constitute four independent confirmations. The seismic intervals also lack correction for the candidate search.

Balanced oil result. The paired mean squared-log-loss difference is +0.0810, with a nominal 95% interval of +0.0648 to +0.0997: the future-time oil comparison favors the baseline. This interval is for squared log loss, not the difference in the RMSLE values shown above.

What is still missing is a complete connection between a specific target, its seismic, measured reservoir properties, and depletion history.

Proposed next step

Start with one agreed field or acquisition package.

A small dataset is enough to scope the first test. We would agree on the permitted use and the question worth answering before accessing or training on proprietary data.

01 / Scope and permission

Define the target and the allowed use

Field or package boundary, relevant dates, available files, and explicit permission for the agreed analysis and model training.

02 / Seismic and well ties

Connect the survey to the wells

Available seismic, survey geometry, well logs, and checkshot or velocity information that can connect time, depth, and formation.

03 / Reservoir calibration

Use measured rock and fluid properties

Formation-specific net pay, porosity, saturation, and fluid measurements, with their source and uncertainty.

04 / Depletion and operating history

Account for what has already happened

Allocated production, pressure and injection histories, plus the interventions and operating context needed to interpret them.

Agree on the test before the answer

Freeze the decision date, hold out a field or target, and compare an engineering baseline with and without the additional data. Review the error, uncertainty, and practical usefulness with High River’s team.

Keep the data under agreed terms

The proposed commitment leaves ownership with the data owner: approved internal use, no resale, and no external training upload or release of proprietary data or trained models without written permission.

The pilot should earn a specific, measured claim before acquisition use. Remaining oil in place, technically recoverable oil, economic reserves, and purchase value are separate questions; each needs its own evidence.
