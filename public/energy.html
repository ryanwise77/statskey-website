The principle

Why investment comes in steps.

Uncertainty is the cost driver in every drilling and acquisition decision. The rational response is not one big bet — it is a sequence of smaller ones, each sized to the uncertainty that remains, and each capable of changing the decision that follows it.

01

Screen wide, spend little

Public records make the first pass nearly free. A ranked list narrows thousands of candidates to a testable few — before any capital is at risk.

02

Test where the answer is known

Locked forecasts are scored against wells with recorded outcomes. A cheap test that can change an expensive decision is the best money in the program.

03

Commit as evidence compounds

Capital steps up only when the previous stage's forecasts held. Each tier of spend is justified by the uncertainty the last tier removed — never by the promise of the next.

Information is only worth buying when it can change a decision — so the cheap information comes first, and the size of every bet follows the strength of the record, not the strength of the story.

Modeled capital return

At modeled pricing and margin, a first tranche is returned by roughly four to six paid engagements — before any success-fee upside on the drilling and acquisition decisions the work informs.

Realistically expecting a return starts near $75,000 — and none of it buys hardware. The $40,000 infrastructure is already purchased, already trained the current model, and is already running around the clock: founder capital, spent before asking for anyone else's. The tranche funds roughly $10,000 of operations and rented burst compute for a year, with the balance delivering the first scored pilots through to completion — which is when the return clock starts.

The return arrives on two tracks. Retainers on screening and backtest engagements are priced with margin by construction — five-figure engagements against low-four-figure delivery cost — and return the tranche as cash. Success interests on decisions that proceed carry the upside: the work informs $250,000 workovers and $2,000,000 wells, and a small interest in a small number of good outcomes is where the multiple lives. The spend side stays disciplined — training stops when the measured learning curve flattens, and at current prices each GPU pays back against renting in 11–21 months.

Scale multiplies the same arithmetic; it does not change it. The corpus and the trained model are built once and reused by every engagement that follows, so delivery cost per engagement falls as the operation grows — margin widens with scale rather than compressing. A scaled configuration — a multi-GPU tier and a few hundred terabytes of storage, roughly $60,000–$120,000 of further infrastructure at 2026 prices — supports one to two engagements a month across multiple districts: a mid-six-figure annual run-rate at the margins above, on infrastructure that costs less per year than one month of the revenue it carries. The growing book of success interests sits on top, each tied to decisions ten to a hundred times the size of the fee that created it. And the margin is defended by construction: pricing is set cost-plus at every tier, and because the cost base is owned, this year's cloud and hardware price inflation raised competitors' delivery costs — not ours.

The bigger number

The seven-figure value sits on the operator's side of the table.

This model is deliberately small on our side — fees with margin, plus small success interests. The larger arithmetic belongs to the asset owner, because the value shows up as redirected capital on wells and deals they already control.

01

Workover triage at field scale

Across a thousand legacy wellbores, surfacing ten economic workovers a year at a $250,000 average cost, each returning about twice its cost, creates roughly $2.5M of annual value on assets already owned. Our share is the fee and a small interest; the rest stays in the field.

02

Acquisition underwriting

On producing-asset deals priced in the tens of millions, data-complete diligence that moves price or catches one bad assumption is worth 1–2% of deal size — $500,000 to $1,000,000 on a single $50M transaction, recurring with deal cadence.

03

Plug-or-justify triage

Every legacy wellbore justified rather than plugged avoids a $30,000–$100,000 plugging liability. Across the hundreds of wells facing that decision in a mature field, the triage alone is a seven-figure line.

The bridge to scale is pre-agreed and evidence-gated: fund the pilot at five figures now, with larger participation locked to a scored backtest on wells whose outcomes are already known. If the top decile doesn't beat the operator's own history, the option never triggers — our staging principle, applied to ourselves.

The rule

Prove it before capital follows.

Lock each forecast before the well result. Compare it with the current workflow on later wells and areas the model has never seen. Use it only if it picks better wells or forecasts cost and production better after data and operating costs.

Intelligence can improve the odds. It cannot remove geological, execution, price, title, safety, or regulatory risk.
