The principle

Generalization beats interpretation.

Superior data, not cleverer features. A model trained on a representative sample of the earth's subsurface learns structure that transfers — the same way large models generalize in every other domain.

One field teaches one field; a thousand surveys teach the rock. The scale and breadth of representative data is the moat — not any single clever model.

Compute is the enabler, not the edge. Modern accelerators are simply what finally make training on a corpus this large possible.

Majors aim this at their own acreage. No one aims it at the whole public record.

Everyone models their own acreage. No model predicts the whole record.

The method

A disciplined analog engine — with memory and calibration.

A geologist compares a prospect to nearby wells, logs, seismic character, and production analogs. The model does the same across millions of examples — then tests whether those similarities would actually have predicted the outcome before it happened.

Separate features from labels. Use only what was knowable before the bit — seismic, maps, offset logs, basin context — to predict what came after: IP, cumulative production, dry hole, payout. The model only gets credit for what it could have known at decision time.

Learn rock patterns, not just locations. Proximity is only the baseline. The edge is seismic texture, impedance-style attributes, log-derived rock properties, structure, facies, and completion context — because rock physics transfers across counties and leases where coordinates don't.

Normalize by geology. Don't compare a Kansas IP to a Gulf Coast cumulative. Ask how good a well is for its own basin, play, and vintage — so the model learns relative quality across depositional systems and technology eras.

Hold out space and time. Train on older wells and other areas, then predict newer wells and whole regions the model never saw — the same conditions a real capital decision has to survive.

Update with outcomes. Every new well, recompletion, failure, and production history is another calibration point. The corpus compounds — the model learns which signals were real and which were noise.

The goal isn't perfect prediction. It's better ranking and better calibration — fewer low-probability wells funded, more high-probability intervals pursued, and more honest downside cases.

The economics

What a globally generalizable model actually costs.

Global generalization doesn't begin until the model trains across a representative, petabyte-scale slice of the record — anything smaller is a regional prototype. The data already sits at that scale: Norway's Diskos alone holds 22 PB and Brazil's BDEP 11.6 PB of public petroleum data. The gating cost was never licensing the rock — it's the compute and storage to learn from petabytes of it.

$1–5M

Compute · train and re-train

One TGS-class run — a 1.8B-parameter model over 444,000 km² — is now ~15,000 GPU-hours, about 5 days on 128 H200s (≈$45k). A global model is larger and spans 50–250× the data, trained and ablated many times: a program in the hundreds of thousands to low-millions of GPU-hours.

$70–280k

Storage · per petabyte, per year

Keeping a petabyte online runs ~$7/TB·mo on Wasabi or Backblaze up to ~$23/TB·mo on S3 — and you hold it several times over: raw SEG-Y, analysis-ready cubes, and model checkpoints, co-located with the GPUs.

~$90k

Data movement · per petabyte

The record is mostly free to license — but moving and converting it isn't. Egress runs up to ~$90k per petabyte off hyperscaler-hosted archives, before converting SEG-Y into analysis-ready cubes and tying seismic to wells and production outcomes.
