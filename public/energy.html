The principle

Why investment comes in steps.

Uncertainty is the cost driver in every drilling and acquisition decision. The rational response is not one big bet — it is a sequence of smaller ones, each sized to the uncertainty that remains, and each capable of changing the decision that follows it.

01

Screen wide, spend little

Public records make the first screen cheap. A ranked list narrows thousands of candidates to a testable few — before any capital is at risk.

02

Test where the answer is known

Locked forecasts are scored against wells with recorded outcomes. A cheap test that can change an expensive decision is the best money in the program.

03

Commit as evidence compounds

Capital steps up only when the previous stage's forecasts held. Each step of spending is justified by what the last step proved — not by the promise of the next.

Information is only worth buying when it can change a decision — so the cheap information comes first, and each bet is sized to the evidence, not the story.

01

A ranking is only worth what it changes

If a ranking tells you to drill what you would have drilled anyway, it is worth nothing. If it is no better than a coin flip, it is worth nothing. Once a ranking beats chance, its value climbs quickly: a test that is right nine times in ten captures more than half the value of knowing the answer perfectly. The gain on each decision is a dry hole avoided or a good well found, multiplied by the number of decisions you make.

Source: Bratvold, Bickel & Lohne, value of information (SPE 110378) ↗

02

Reliability has to be measured, not claimed

That calculation needs one number the industry rarely measures: how often the prediction is right. Of 25 published studies on the value of such predictions, only 13 stated it at all, and 11 of those used expert opinion. Forecasts also run high — fields deliver about a fifth less than forecast in their first five years. Scoring the model on wells whose results are already known, with those results hidden from it, replaces a claim with a measurement. Your own record is the minimum standard any model must beat.

Source: Bratvold, Bickel & Lohne, value of information (SPE 110378) ↗ · Nesvold & Bratvold, study of production forecasts ↗

03

What the model learns in one field carries to the next — and we measure how much

The model is trained first on the broad public record of seismic, well measurements, and production, so it does not start from zero on your field. A model prepared that way and then adjusted with about a hundred local examples beat one built from scratch by a wide margin. A model trained on the rest of a basin cut forecast error 30–48% in counties with only 12–36 wells and no local training. What carries is the relationship between what is measured and what comes out of the ground, not the map location. Moving to a new basin costs some accuracy — about ten percentage points on one geological-mapping task in the published test. We measure and report that cost rather than assume it.

04

One model trained on all your data beats a separate model per asset

In a hydrology study, one model trained on 531 river basins beat models built one basin at a time. In oil, a model trained across several basins beat standard decline forecasts by 23% on undrilled locations and 36% on producing wells, and helped most where a basin’s data was thin. So the question is never which asset to model; it is how much of your own record to pool. The regulator’s record is public; partner data can be included where your agreements allow. Training repeatedly on the same data stops helping after a few rounds; adding new data keeps helping.

The math, in plain words measured in oil and seismic where marked · otherwise from general machine-learning scaling studies
01

Error has two parts

A part no model can remove — what the inputs do not contain — and a part that shrinks as the model sees more examples and is trained harder. Everything below is about the second part.

02

More examples: the big lever

Each doubling of the wells the model learns from removes roughly 5–10% of the removable error. Measured in oil: a model that went from about 360 to 900 wells lost a 20% forecast bias entirely.

03

Harder training: the steady lever

Each tenfold increase in computing removes a few percent of the removable error — about 1–3% per doubling — and stops helping once the data has been learned: a few passes over the same data are nearly free; beyond about sixteen they add nothing. Measured in seismic: interpretation error halves for each elevenfold increase when model and data grow together.

04

The base model multiplies your data

Because the model has already learned the public record, each of your wells teaches it more than it would teach a fresh model — roughly 30% more value per doubling of the base model’s size. This is why the smallest engagement already works and why the second field costs less than the first.

05

So the order is fixed

First dollars: put all of your data in one model — the large step. Next dollars: train it harder — small, steady, measured steps. We measure the slope on your own wells and stop when it flattens.

06

Error into money

A ranking is worth what it changes: each one-point improvement in the chance a well pays, times the gap between a good well and a dry one, times the wells it touches. Lower error means fewer bad wells drilled, and the good ones drilled first.

01

Where to land

Physics: thickness and porosity from seismic inversion; structure and dip from the reflectors; where fractures open from the elastic properties. Statistics: offsets with known landings give the effect size — in one basin study, about +5% to −10% of three-year oil by position in the zone (model-inferred). We supply the quality map along the planned path with ranges; the engineer chooses the target.

02

How to complete

Physics: fracture geometry follows pumped volume against stress; productivity scales with conductive area until fractures interfere — returns diminish. Measured: grouping stages by similar stress raised cluster efficiency from 64% to 82% in one program; optima differ by sub-area; operators have captured about two-thirds of the profit available from their input choices. We supply the marginal-return curve and its knee for your rock; the engineer sets the design.

03

How close to the next well

Physics: an existing well has drawn down the pressure around it, so a child well inside that drainage recovers less. Measured: up to about 40% below unbounded parents, and the penalty depends on completion size, so spacing and intensity are chosen together. We supply the expected penalty per candidate, with a range.

04

Steering while drilling

Physics: the seismic model predicts the layer geometry ahead of the bit; logging-while-drilling corrects it — a prior and a posterior. Re-deciding as information arrives beats deciding once. Frontier, stated plainly: real-time geosteering is commercial but has published no metrics, and no published work yet feeds seismic-model representations into steering — we score it rather than assume it.

05

What the well will make

Measured: a model that has learned the record forecasts a planned well with 15% error against 20% for planning type curves on held-out wells, with ranges that cover what they claim. We supply the range, its reliability, and the ranking of alternatives; the decision and the well are yours.
