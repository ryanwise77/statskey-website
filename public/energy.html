The principle

Why investment comes in steps.

Uncertainty is the cost driver in every drilling and acquisition decision. The rational response is not one big bet — it is a sequence of smaller ones, each sized to the uncertainty that remains, and each capable of changing the decision that follows it.

01

Screen wide, spend little

Public records make the first screen cheap. A ranked list narrows thousands of candidates to a testable few — before any capital is at risk.

02

Test where the answer is known

Locked forecasts are scored against wells with recorded outcomes. A cheap test that can change an expensive decision is the best money in the program.

03

Commit as evidence compounds

Capital steps up only when the previous stage's forecasts held. Each tier of spend is justified by the uncertainty the last tier removed — never by the promise of the next.

Information is only worth buying when it can change a decision — so the cheap information comes first, and the size of every bet follows the strength of the record, not the strength of the story.
The order

Run the history first. Earn the upside on what it measures.

Every well you drill and every asset you price is already a bet on a probability; the only open question is how well that probability is measured. Your own history — every well, log, survey, workover, and recompletion, with its outcome — is the one instrument that measures how reliable any ranking is on your rock, and that measurement, not the model, sets what the upside is worth. That record already exists, alongside the offset and regulator data you hold rights to. Running it through the model — field by field, rung by rung — turns a claim into a confusion matrix.

Oil and gas pays the people who de-risk capital on the outcome, not by the hour — the prospect generator keeps an override, the underwriter takes points — and StatsKey prices the same way: cash for the work now, participation in outcomes after the backtest clears, on a schedule agreed before it is scored. The economics underneath are owned, not rented: corpus, model, and machines are built once and run around the clock on dedicated hardware.

01

A ranking is worth what it changes

EMV = p·S + (1−p)·F · VOI = E[ max over actions, with the signal ] − max over actions, without · 0 ≤ VOI ≤ EVPI

A ranking earns its value on the decisions it changes; one with coin-flip reliability changes none and is worth nothing. Above that threshold the value rises steeply — in the SPE worked example a 90%-reliable test captures 57% of the value of perfect information. The ceiling per decision is the dry hole avoided or the good well captured, times the decisions you make under a budget.

Sources: Bratvold, Bickel & Lohne, SPE 110378 ↗ · Pickering & Bickel, value of seismic (OGFJ 2006) ↗

02

Reliability is measured on your own history, not claimed

P(x | y) ∝ P(y | x) · P(x) — the likelihood P(y | x) is the confusion matrix from scoring decisions whose outcomes are already known · SE ∝ 1/√n

Every value-of-information calculation needs one number the industry rarely measures: how often the signal is right. In the SPE survey of imperfect-information studies, thirteen of twenty-five assessed reliability at all — eleven by expert judgment, two by tying it to a model — and published studies find fields delivering about 20% less than forecast in their first five years. Running the wells in scope through the model, outcomes held out by spatial block, turns a claimed reliability into a measured one; and in the published scaling fits an order of magnitude of unique data moves error further than an order of magnitude of compute — your record sets the floor.

Sources: SPE 110378 ↗ · Nesvold & Bratvold, debiasing production forecasts (Energy 2022, via FORCE 2024) ↗ · Kaplan et al., scaling laws ↗

03

What one field teaches is expected to carry — the broad record multiplies your labels, and the carry is scored, not assumed

D_eff = D_F · [ 1 + k·N^β · D_F^(α−1) ] — pretraining multiplies local labels most when they are few · ε_target ≤ ε_source + d(source, target) + λ

Pretrained on the broad seismic, log, and production record, the model multiplies a field’s labeled wells rather than learning from zero: a seismic foundation model fine-tuned on about a hundred local labels scored 0.73 mIoU against 0.49 from scratch, and a production model trained on the rest of a basin’s counties cut forecast error 30–48% in five thin counties with 12–36 wells each and no local training. What carries is the physical relationship between measurement and outcome — reflectivity to rock and fluid, pressure and permeability to decline — not addresses. Domain-adaptation theory says the penalty for moving is bounded and measurable; the published number is about ten points on a structural task (foreground IoU 0.83 against 0.93 on a third basin, after fine-tuning on two).

Sources: Hernandez et al., transfer scaling ↗ · Sheng et al., Seismic Foundation Model ↗ · MIT, cross-county transfer ↗ · TGS, First Break 2025 ↗

04

Coverage: pooling the record has beaten one model per asset wherever it has been tested

error(one model, all sites) < error(per-site models), on average across sites · D′ = U · [ 1 + R*(1 − e^(−R/R*)) ], R* ≈ 15 — repetition can make a fixed archive worth at most ≈16× its unique size, each pass worth less than the last

One model trained on 531 catchments beat models calibrated one catchment at a time; in oil and gas, a multi-basin model beat decline curves by about 23% on undrilled locations and 36% on producing wells, and pooling basins helped most where a basin’s data was thin. So the question is never which asset to model but how much of your own record to pool — the regulator record is public, and partner data follows your agreements. A fixed archive can be learned only so many times: a few passes are nearly free, by tens of passes nothing; each new, different record moves the floor in a way more passes cannot.

Sources: Kratzert et al., HESS 2019 ↗ · Amr et al., SPE 191538 ↗ · Muennighoff et al., data-constrained scaling ↗

05

Applied to the drilling program and to acquisitions

∂EMV/∂p = S − F → N · (S − F) · Δp across a program · logit p_post = logit p_prior + evidence from each result · A&D: overpayment grows with valuation-error variance

Each point of realized success probability is worth the full spread between a good well and a dry one, on every well drilled — on a program of dozens of wells a year, that spread times the wells is what the upside is priced against; the fee is set by scope. Re-deciding as results post is worth more than one-off scoring: in a 2026 case study, a two-stage plan that re-decided after first results raised expected NPV 34% and cut tail loss 59% over a one-shot plan, with Bayesian updating of the success probabilities adding a few percent more. In acquisitions the same calibrated model lowers the variance of your valuation error — which is what the winner’s curse taxes.

Sources: Bickel & Smith, optimal sequential exploration (Decision Analysis 2006) ↗ · Willigers, Begg & Bratvold, SPE 164816-PA ↗ · Portfolio recourse case study, 2026 ↗

06

Compute scales with the data, not ahead of it

L(N, D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β · reducible loss ∝ C^(−0.15 … −0.18)

Compute-optimal training grows the model and the data in step — which is why the compute share rises gently with scope: preparation and review do not scale with the record, but the model’s appetite does. Returns are a power law — tenfold compute buys roughly a third of the remaining error under the published fits, tenfold unique data buys more — and repeated passes over a fixed archive stop moving the curve, so training stops when the curve flattens and spend follows evidence, not a budget line. Published seismic pretraining runs, from one basin’s archive to a 63-survey global corpus, cost roughly 1,000 to 24,000 GPU-hours; the basin rung covers that several times over.

Sources: Hoffmann et al., Chinchilla ↗ · Epoch AI, replication ↗ · NCS-Model, 2026 ↗ · TGS, First Break 2025 ↗

07

Upside is set against the measured reliability — agreed before scoring, paid after

buy information iff VOI(r) > price + delay · r measured on held-out history → VOI(r) computed → the schedule agreed before scoring is filled in

The participation schedule — what share follows what measured reliability — is agreed in writing alongside the pass bar, before anything is scored; the backtest then fills in the number. A share set without that number is a share of a guess; a share negotiated after it is a negotiation about sunk work. Nothing is paid before the bar clears — commit as evidence compounds.

Sources: SPE 110378 ↗

Where the fee goes, and why the compute share rises

Compute is what turns the record into a calibrated ranking. What the model learns is the physics in the record — how reflectivity answers to rock and fluid, how pressure and permeability set a well’s decline, how a completion responds to the rock it meets — and the published scaling laws say model and data should grow together: more record ingested justifies more of it learned, finer models, more blind wells scored, retraining as each result posts. At the baseline engagement, sixty cents of every dollar runs as that compute, dedicated to your program — owned GPUs and storage first, rented bursts when a test justifies them. The share rises modestly with scope, because preparation and review do not scale with the size of the record while the model’s appetite does; and it is bounded, because repeated passes over a fixed archive stop moving the curve, so training ends when the curve flattens. The rest pays for preparation, review, and named engineering sign-off on every refresh.

At a district, about two-thirds of every dollar is compute; at a basin, seven in ten; at a portfolio, three-quarters.

The physics is what carries. Seismic reflectivity answers to rock and fluid the same way in every district; decline is set by pressure and permeability everywhere; a completion responds to the rock it meets. The model learns those relationships from the record — not addresses — which is why one field’s calibration is expected to carry, why pooling beats one model per asset, and why the second district costs less than the first. More blind wells shrink the error bar on the reliability — roughly with the square root of their number, less where wells are clustered.

Half a million is a complete basin program: enough passes to reach the floor the basin’s data sets, with blind tests at every refresh; five million runs the same program across everything you operate, weekly. GPU-hours are H100-equivalent. Every engagement opens at the bottom rung, credited against the next; the participation schedule is agreed up front and paid after the bar clears.

What the improvement is worth, at stated assumptions — a forty-well program, $2M wells, about $5M of three-year revenue per well: re-siting or deferring a bottom decile that underperforms by 15% is about $3M of revenue; removing a −20% systematic bias is about $40M of forecast error on that revenue base, a budget and reserves number rather than cash; moving landings from the bottom half of the zone to the top is roughly $8–18M a year; and on exploration, each point of realized success probability is worth the spread between the success case and the dry hole on every well — (S − F)·Δp. The figures are priors from published results — several vendor-authored, several model-inferred without confidence intervals — and your own blind backtest replaces them with the measured number. The model is not a deliverable

Outputs are yours — rankings, forecasts, evidence files — to keep and use. The base model, corpus, and methods are ours, including what we learn about how to train. What the model learns from your data is held for your engagement and is not carried into anyone else’s.

The backtest is the first deliverable

It is priced, scored, and credited against the next rung. Fees buy compute and review, not hours — the way the industry pays the people who de-risk capital.

Your data stays yours

Operator data is used only to deliver that operator’s engagement; it trains nothing used for anyone else. Any broader use would require a separate, written data-rights agreement. Exclusivity is not included; basin- or area-level exclusivity is available as a priced term.

Transfer and scaling figures above are published results from adjacent domains — seismic interpretation, production forecasting, hydrology, and language-model scaling — not a decision-ranking benchmark; the backtest fits your own curve, and carry is measured on held-out blocks, never assumed. The bigger number

The seven-figure value sits on the operator's side of the table.

The structure is deliberately asymmetric: our side is the spend now and a share sized to the measured lift, after proof. The larger arithmetic belongs to the asset owner, because the value shows up as redirected capital on wells and deals they already control.

Workover triage at field scale

Across a thousand legacy wellbores, surfacing ten economic workovers a year at a $250,000 average cost, each returning about twice its cost, creates roughly $2.5M of annual value on assets already owned. Our share is the fee and, after proof, an interest sized to the measured lift; the rest stays in the field.

Acquisition underwriting

On producing-asset deals priced in the tens of millions, data-complete diligence that moves price or catches one bad assumption is worth 1–2% of deal size — $500,000 to $1,000,000 on a single $50M transaction, recurring with deal cadence.

03

Plug-or-justify triage

Every legacy wellbore justified rather than plugged avoids a $30,000–$100,000 plugging liability. Across the hundreds of wells facing that decision in a mature field, the triage alone is a seven-figure line.

The rule

Prove it before capital follows.

Lock each forecast before the well result. Compare it with the current workflow on later wells and areas the model has never seen. Use it only if it picks better wells or forecasts cost and production better after data and operating costs.

Intelligence can improve the odds. It cannot remove geological, execution, price, title, safety, or regulatory risk.
